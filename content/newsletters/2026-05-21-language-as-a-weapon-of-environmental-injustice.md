## Language as a Weapon of Environmental Injustice

While listening to Jenni Doering’s forthcoming interview with Theonila Roka Matbob, winner of the 2026 [Goldman Environmental Prize](https://www.goldmanprize.org/recipient/theonila-roka-matbob/) for Islands and Island nations, I thought I knew the story we were trying to cover. 

 

As a producer, it’s my job to read up on a subject before we interview a guest. This way, I can craft questions that drive us towards the heart of an issue. And in this case, I had read all about Theonila and her fight for justice in her native island of Bougainville, an autonomous region in Papua New Guinea. 

 

From 1972 to 1989, the Panguna mine, run by the multinational company Rio Tinto, turned a once-self-sustaining ecosystem into a mess of toxic rivers and polluted farmland. There has never been a proper clean up by Rio Tinto, leaving the indigenous population to suffer grave health consequences for decades. 

 

Born in 1990, Theonila grew up witnessing her community’s health struggles and began to question why nothing had been done to remediate the pollution left by Rio Tinto. So in 2019, in collaboration with Human Rights Law Centre, Theonila led a grassroots campaign to document community testimonies and accuse Rio Tinto of human rights and environmental abuses. As of 2024, Rio Tinto has agreed to a non-binding “Memorandum of Understanding” to address some of the most urgent  impacts of the mine, like clean water access for nearby communities. While the memorandum is a start, Theonila says that the repercussions of Rio Tinto’s operations will require the company to make an even more comprehensive, long-term commitment to the community’s health, social, and environmental needs.  

 

This was the story I expected to hear about during our call. And it was, for the most part. 

 

But then, Theonila said something that struck me:  

 

**“Rio Tinto, when it went onto the land, it used the language as a weapon.”**

 

Rio Tinto, the second largest mining company in the world, is Anglo-Australian. English is its primary language of communication. 

 

Bougainville, on the other hand, is home to over a dozen distinct indigenous languages and dialects. And while English is used in the region for official government and business purposes, Theonila explained that the vast majority of her people have limited access to education, so fluency in English is rare. Rio Tinto, she said, didn’t bother to translate any of their official documents into local languages, leaving those most impacted by the mine in the dark. 

 

This was a piece of the story I hadn’t considered. After all, it wasn’t explicitly outlined in any of our media briefing materials. And yet here it was, hiding in plain sight. How had I not seen it?

 

I’ve thought a lot about language barriers this year. I’m currently living in Greece, my dad’s native country, attempting to learn the language and navigate life abroad. It can be an incredibly frustrating process, and I fail often. But here’s the thing: when it gets hard, I can always fall back on English. That’s because, due to a privilege I have earned by no merit of my own, my native tongue remains globally dominant (a consequence primarily of colonialism and economic influence). I may never know what it’s like to feel *truly* powerless in the face of a language that’s not my own. 

 

When it comes to the experience of Bougainvilleans, the consequences of being linguistically excluded have been dire. Residents claim that pregnancy and birth complications like miscarriages, cervical infections, and low-weight newborns are now more common due to the toxic legacy of the mine. Many residents face horrific conditions where their skin literally rots away on the soles of their feet, a health issue that they allege arises from contact with chemicals in polluted rivers. And Bougainvilleans say the island’s soil is also polluted, compromising their ability to grow safe food. 

 

At no point were Bougainvilleans properly consulted about the opening of Panguna mine. And they were not informed, in their own languages, about the potential dangers this kind of operation could pose. Of course, one could argue that it’s unlikely for a company to be forthcoming about their hazardous activities in *any* language. That very well may be true. For example, in the United States, [Bayer and its subsidiary Monsanto have been accused of allegedly failing to adequately warn consumers of the cancer risks of their weedkiller product, Roundup](https://loe.org/shows/segments.html?programID=26-P13-00018\&segmentID=1). But I find it disturbing that a foreign extractive company can still set up shop without engaging in a proper dialogue with the local population. Neocolonialism is alive and well. 

 

The plight of Bougainville got me thinking about environmental injustice through a whole new lens. How many foreign companies have been able to bulldoze their way into other nations by using language “as a weapon?” How many times have indigenous people been shut out from crucial international climate talks because their languages were not centered? What perspectives are we still missing?

 

It will not be easy to fight for the inclusion of linguistic diversity in climate justice conversations. It will not be easy to convince multi-billion dollar companies to make their communications accessible to those who may want to challenge them. But I hope Theonila’s story can serve as a starting point.
