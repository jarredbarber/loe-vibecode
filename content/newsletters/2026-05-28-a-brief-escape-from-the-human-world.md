## A Brief Escape from the Human World

Sometimes we humans can get very absorbed in our own world. Between midterm elections, spreadsheets, and Memorial Day sales, it’s easy to forget that we were once grassland mammals roaming the Earth and picking bugs out of each other’s hair. Modern human life definitely has its perks (shampoo, for one). But when I’m feeling overwhelmed, it can help to slow down, step away from the news cycle, and tune in to the sounds, behaviors, and lives of the non-human creatures who live alongside us. 

 

So let’s take a deeper dive into the animal-centered stories we covered this week on *Living on Earth.*

 

[Right whales](https://loe.org/shows/segments.html?programID=26-P13-00021\&segmentID=2)

It’s easy to love right whales after listening to our interview with New England Aquarium’s Amy Warren. Amy explained that right whales have distinctive callosities, or rough patches of skin on their heads that are actually large colonies of whale lice. And, get this – each individual has a completely unique callosity pattern. I did some digging and discovered that in 2016, researchers at NOAA launched [The Right Whale Recognition competition,](https://www.kaggle.com/competitions/noaa-right-whale-recognition/data) where data scientists competed to build artificial intelligence and facial recognition programs capable of identifying individual whales. Christian Khan, who founded the competition, was reportedly inspired by Facebook's photo-tagging technology. The winning team achieved an 87% accuracy rate matching whales by their callosities. Basically, if right whales had iPhones, Face ID would work almost every time. 

 

[Songbirds](https://loe.org/shows/segments.html?programID=26-P13-00021\&segmentID=3)

If you listened to this week’s BirdNote®, you heard the delightful melody of the black-headed grosbeak, a striking black-and-orange bird in the cardinal family. I did some research and learned that [grosbeak chicks sing and ruffle their feathers during mealtimes](https://www.audubon.org/field-guide/bird/black-headed-grosbeak) to encourage their mothers to share food. Maybe human parents would also appreciate a thank-you song and dance when they place a stack of pancakes on the breakfast table. If anyone reading this decides to try it, let me know how it goes. I also learned that grosbeaks are big fans of grape jelly, which gives them a much-needed sugar boost during migration season. Cue “Espresso” by Sabrina Carpenter.

 

[Self-medicating monkeys](https://loe.org/shows/segments.html?programID=26-P13-00021\&segmentID=4)

You may remember our previous conversation with reporter Teresa Tommasoni about stingless bees – and if not, [it’s a must-listen!](https://www.loe.org/shows/segments.html?programID=25-P13-00046\&segmentID=4) Her reporting stemmed from a trip to the Amazon rainforest with one of this week’s guests, Dr. Rosa Espinoza, whose work preserving Amazonian species blends science with ancestral knowledge. One area of research Dr. Espinoza now focuses on is zoopharmacognosy: the phenomenon of [animals self-medicating](https://www.loe.org/shows/segments.html?programID=25-P13-00032\&segmentID=4) by ingesting or applying specific plants, soils, or insects. For example, Amazonian monkeys like capuchins and titi monkeys rub plants into their fur to ward off pests and treat fungal infections. I’m inspired – maybe I’ll try making a natural bug repellent at home this hiking season. 

So, as you go back to whatever human activity you were doing – skimming emails, most likely – take a lesson or two from the animal kingdom with you throughout the day. And stay tuned for more wild stories from *Living on Earth.*
