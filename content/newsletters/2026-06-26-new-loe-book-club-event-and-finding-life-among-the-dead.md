## Finding Life Among the Dead

A constant joke around the Living on Earth office is that we might as well be called Dying on Earth, with the way in which so much of our coverage focuses on natural disasters, biodiversity loss, and the overall sense of climate dread that pervades our modern society. Even referring to that as a ‘joke’ feels a little wrong, since it’s usually accompanied by the uncomfortable truth that ours is a news cycle dominated by death and destruction. I know we’re not alone in feeling like it’s a little hard to reckon with The Horrors of the present day.

 

And we always like to find the good news when we can: stories of legal wins for climate protection, justice for communities facing environmental racism, or endangered species making a comeback. But we always take care to skirt the line of becoming Pollyannish (another term regularly used by the Living on Earth team, meaning optimistic, but sometimes to the point of naïveté. Fun fact – Pollyanna originates from the [classic children’s novel,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pollyanna) where the titular character Pollyanna tries to find the good in every situation). 

 

But then again, it doesn’t feel so much like ‘good news’ if you immediately try and undercut it with The Horrors. So, instead, I love whenever we get to report something that takes the realities of the world, including all its death and destruction, and says that we don’t need to emphasize the sadness to make sure we seem appropriately somber, or ignore it in favor of keeping a bright outlook. Two examples of this in recent LoE memory come from quite literally finding life flourishing among the dead.

 

In early June, [we spoke with Panayiotis Pafilis,](https://www.loe.org/shows/segments.html?programID=26-P13-00023\&segmentID=2) a professor of animal diversity at the National and Kapodistrian University of Athens. He was part of a team examining ancient Greek archeological sites, the kind most of us only hear about from Greek mythology. These sites included the Acropolis of Athens, and Delphi on Mount Parnassus, locations which were reaching their largest influence around the 6th century B.C.E. At one point, they were bustling cultural centers, but now they might be seen by many as merely ruins, or worse, tourist traps. 

 

But Professor Pafilis explained to me both the cultural importance and the biological importance of these places. Overall, he and his team studied 20 archeological sites. They represent only 0.018% of Greece’s landmass but Pafilis’ research indicates that they hold approximately 11% of the country’s total biodiversity within their borders. Not only was the team astonished by the sheer number of species living in these sites, but they were thrilled to see the same species that played an important role in the mythological stories about those sites, like eagles in Delphi. As the story goes, Zeus wanted to find the center of the earth, so he released two eagles from opposite sides of the world, and they [met in the middle over Delphi.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Omphalos_of_Delphi) So when Professor Pafilis’ team actually saw eagles flying overhead in Delphi, he compared it to having “a window” into the past.

 

Now, that study in Greece deals with ghostly ruins and death from multiple millennia ago, but let’s fast forward to just a few centuries ago. This week, [we spoke with Cornell University professor Bryan Danforth](https://loe.org/shows/segments.html?programID=26-P13-00025\&segmentID=4) about a cemetery buzzing with bees. You might think of graveyards as anything from macabre to mundane, but you probably wouldn’t think of them as lively (aside from the rare [landscape garden cemeter*y full of wildlife*](https://stream.loe.org/shows/segments.html?programID=15-P13-00044\&segmentID=5\&mc_cid=1c49e8f6b4\&mc_eid=UNIQID)). But the Cornell research team found that their local cemetery in Ithaca, New York had around 5.6 million bees living in its boundaries. In fact, they’re so prevalent that the site first sparked the curiosity of one of the researchers involved in the study when she came across “clouds” of bees flying around the cemetery on her way into work. 

 

To put that in context, that’s about 270 honeybee colonies – and to put THAT in context, that’s about four times the human population of Manhattan. And all those bees are hugely important pollinators, helping to keep New York State the second-largest producer of apples in the country. But if you have a fear of stinging honeybees, there’s no need to worry – these are [solitary bees,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Andrena_regularis) who nest in the ground and are much more docile than their more famous cousins.

 

In both of these circumstances, it was no mere coincidence that life was flourishing in these areas filled with death – death was what made life possible. In the Greek study, the archeological sites were protected since the early 19th century, keeping them safe from hunting, urban development, and humans running about willy-nilly. Professor Pafilis framed it as sort of a meeting point between natural conservation and historical preservation. 

 

And in New York, the Ithaca cemetery was built on sandy loam. That’s a type of soil that makes it really easy to dig, and therefore, really easy to bury human remains. But that sandy loam is also a perfect habitat for these solitary bees who need to dig in order to nest in the ground. So the qualities that made the area suitable for death also made it suitable for life.

 

Hearing these two stories left me feeling very in-touch with the circle of life. With life, comes death, and life again. The good will always exist alongside the bad. And ultimately, I feel we can navigate our world more fully when we remember to seek out not just good news, or bad news, but the realities that include all of the above.

**Aynsley O'Neill**

Producer
