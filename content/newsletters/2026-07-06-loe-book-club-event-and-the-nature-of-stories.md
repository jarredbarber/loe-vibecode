## The Nature of Stories: A Reader's Journey to Living on Earth

Hello listeners!

 

My name is Abby Edgecumbe, and I am interning at Living on Earth this summer. I’m so happy to introduce myself to you all. I am a reader by nature, but I’ve always felt drawn to the natural world and felt a fierce desire to protect it. I’ve followed paths as diverse as academia, librarianship, and now public radio trying to bridge those two passions. Sometimes it feels like I’ve been trying to build that bridge since I was little.

 

I’m from a coastal New England town, a place where kids grow up a little bit feral and salty– I rowed a storage crate, pilfered from my parents’ garage, out to sea as a kid. In such environments, you start to read your surroundings. Not only reading the temperature and humidity for weather predictions, but reading stories into what you see around you; hearing tales in the herons’ call as they swoop over the marshlands, or reading fables in the woody chords of the trees. As I built my own stories, running around like Huck Finn or Nancy Drew, my love of the outdoors began to reflect in my love of reading, and vice versa. As such, I majored in English in undergrad, at California Polytechnic State University in San Luis Obispo, and then went on to earn an MA in Literature from UMASS Amherst. 

 

At UMASS, I focused on the environmental humanities and was introduced to climate fiction. Since finding cli-fi, I rarely read anything else (in fact, most books are climate fiction, whether they know it or not, but that’s just my own hot take, which you can read more about in the book [*Climate Lyricism*](http://bookshop.org/a/20556/9781478017738) by Min Hyoung Song). Experiencing different climate futures through stories gives shape to all the science and policy, making the future feel less vague and scary. Writer and eco-critic Amitav Ghosh writes about that phenomenon in his book [*The Great Derangement: Climate Change and the Unthinkable*](http://bookshop.org/a/20556/9780226526812), writing that “the great, irreplaceable potentiality of fiction is that it makes possible the imagining of possibilities.” Fiction is a place where we can workshop what the future might look like. I even used to run a climate fiction book club at my local library. I really love it, if you can’t tell!

 

After finishing my degree at UMASS, I knew a handful of things: I’m passionate about the environment, about community, and about reading and literacy. Public librarianship emerged as the right path for me to synthesize all my interests, and I’m now enrolled to earn a Master of Library and Information Science at Simmons University. If there’s anything you learn in library school, it’s that libraries aren’t about reading all day; they’re about access to information. As the great Toni Morrison once [said](https://www.nypl.org/blog/2022/09/14/truth-trouble-toni-morrisons-advocacy-against-censorship), “access to knowledge is the superb, the supreme act of truly great civilizations. Of all the institutions that purport to do this, free libraries stand virtually alone in accomplishing this mission.” 

 

I would add public media to her claim. Public libraries and public media share the same heart: the desire to inform the people, to amplify truth, and to make knowledge accessible. As far as the climate is concerned, public libraries have an important role to play– both in the information landscape and in community infrastructure. Public librarians have an obligation to combat misinformation– factually incorrect information– as well as disinformation– factually incorrect information that is maliciously spread to obscure the truth. The spread of both misinformation and disinformation makes tackling climate change all that more difficult. As community infrastructure, public libraries can model sustainability for their communities, by hosting experts, running workshops, or offering materials in their Library of Things (such as seed libraries). In all these ways, libraries are capable of being active players in the fight against climate change. And if I could get more people to read climate fiction along the way, then that’s a bonus.

The main takeaway from *Finding the Mother Tree&#x20;*&#x69;s that the largest and often oldest trees, the ‘mother trees,’ communicate with the rest of the forest through underground mycorrhizal networks of mycelial fungi. Through these networks, mother trees nurture and support the growth of the forest, allocating nutrients to trees in need, and essentially supporting the flourishing of the entire forest system. Sounds a lot like a mother to me!

 

In her new book *When the Forest Breathes*, Dr. Simard takes care to amplify Indigenous perspectives as she further explores the role of the mother tree in forest resiliency. *When the Forest Breathes* centers the cycle of reciprocity and renewal in the forest, highlighting the ways that a forest regenerates itself– if humans align their extraction practices with the forest’s needs. She says, for example, that if we leave a handful of mother trees intact after logging, a forest regenerates at a remarkably fast rate. Such an amazing feat of regeneration, she says, “is the plant and animal and soil world doing its thing, as long as we do the right thing by it.” I, too, want to do the right thing by the natural world. While I am not called to be a forest ecologist, I am called to share stories that uplift and educate people about nature and the climate.

 

Looking back at my upbringing, I see how reading has always been intertwined with the natural world for me. Even now, reading feels like something that has roots in my history, and blossoms when I take care of it. When we read a book, its physical pages implore us to remember how much our lives rely on the natural world, and to do what we can to protect the environment. 

 

At Living on Earth, I am able to champion the stories and thinkers that inspire me. I hope you all continue to listen and enjoy. It’s my pleasure to play a part in bringing you the best in environmental journalism this summer, and to share a little bit of my Living on Earth story with you today.

 

Onwards!

**Abby Edgecumbe**

Editorial Intern
