## I Followed a Drought. It Led Me Across the World.

*Xin chào thính giả của Living on Earth!&#xA0;*

Hello *Living on Earth* listeners!

I’m Nhung Nguyen, an intern this summer.

I’m from Vietnam, a recent agricultural powerhouse of the world. There’s a high chance that the rice, coffee, fruits, and shrimp on your dining table were grown by our farmers, most likely those living in the downstream delta of the Mekong River. In the years between the 1990s and 2000s, this verdant region lifted Vietnam from a war-ravaged, starving country to a major food exporter — a vital link in the global supply chain. Our zero-to-hero journey has been my source of curiosity and fascination, first as a kid, and then a journalist.

To be very frank, though, journalism was not part of my original plan. I studied diplomacy at university, charmed by an imagined life of cake-filled soirees and polite sparring that starts with “With all due respect.” But then I got to read a lot of news pieces and political analyses as part of our class reading, and somewhere in between those pages, I fell in love with this realm full of truth-seeking thrills and deadline-chasing chaos. Later, when I became a journalist myself, every assignment felt like an adventure. I often joke with my friends that this is the only job that keeps my brain from turning into a tofu block.

It took me a few years into my career to feel drawn to climate and environment stories in Vietnam. In 2019, the Mekong Delta endured [a historic drought](https://reliefweb.int/report/viet-nam/vietnam-drought-and-saltwater-intrusion-emergency-plan-action-epoa-dref-operation-n), followed by a severe salt intrusion from *Bien Dong&#x20;*— our East Sea — that threatened the livelihoods of hundreds of thousands of farmers. “Climate change” and “food insecurity” quickly became buzz words. Alongside other local reporters, I roamed around the delta, determined to capture the face of climate change: its damaging impacts, its human cost, its scale. 

Instead, I discovered the story was far more complicated.

Driving past parched paddy fields, talking to the local people and scientists, I learned nature alone was not to blame. Long before its unusual wrath, our industrial production of food has tired our land, dwindling it into a body too weak to withstand that strong wind. 

For decades, we have engineered one of the most fertile regions on Earth into a “food basket” — damming the Mekong River for hydropower, dredging channels and building dykes, pouring in synthetic fertilizers and pesticides to grow our crops year-round. We altered our rivers and soil, confident that abundance would always be ours. Then arrived the punishing drought and rising sea, compounded by upstream dams, unravelling both the belief and the costly system built upon it. 

Even then, climate change refused to fit in a single narrative: it didn’t deal everyone a losing card. While durian growers had to chop down their dying cash trees, grape farmers, just a province over, won record harvests from the very same drought. It wore different faces, depending where you stood.

And I soon realized Vietnam was not alone. 

During the COVID-19 pandemic, I started to listen to podcasts for hours everyday. While friends learned to bake sourdough during shutdowns, I invested in a sewing machine and needed something to accompany my ears when I tried to make my first DIY curtains. That was when I discovered *Living on Earth.* The program quickly became one of my windows to the world. From its episodes, I recognized the same patterns I had seen in Vietnam unfolding everywhere, from [livestock herders in the Horn of Africa](https://www.loe.org/shows/segments.html?programID=22-P13-00030\&segmentID=2) to [soybean and corn growers by the Mississippi River](https://www.loe.org/shows/segments.html?programID=22-P13-00048\&segmentID=3). I even relied on LoE’s report on [climate displacement](https://www.loe.org/shows/segments.html?programID=20-P13-00002\&segmentID=2) while preparing to interview [elderly farmers](https://www.mekongeye.com/2022/11/28/elderly-climate-change) who had abandoned their withered land in the Mekong Delta to look for jobs in bigger cities. Later, when I expanded my reporting on solutions to heat-trapping houses in these migrant magnets in Vietnam, I found an example in Boston’s [green cooling initiatives for public housing](https://loe.org/shows/segments.html?programID=24-P13-00006\&segmentID=1). The more I listened, the more I recognized how interconnected climate stories are, and how much we could learn from each other to navigate this changing planet. 

Last year, I finally had the chance to expand my “classroom” to the other side of the world. Under the support of the Fulbright Program, I came to the U.S. to pursue a master’s degree in Science, Health, and Environmental Reporting at New York University. My sewing machine stayed behind in Vietnam, but *Living on Earth* remained part of my daily routine. When I learned that my favorite program was looking for an intern this summer, applying felt inevitable. 

Last week marks a major milestone for me: the first *Living on Earth* episode I helped produce, not just listen to. It’s fitting that the segment tackles a familiar subject: climate change — how Antarctica is losing its sea ice due to warm air from the South Pacific and warm ocean currents. And again, the lesson is complexity. 

[We talked to Ted Scambos](https://loe.org/shows/segments.html?programID=26-P13-00028\&segmentID=1), a senior research scientist at the Cooperative Institute for Research in Environmental Sciences at the University of Colorado, who has spent years monitoring the poles and their glaciers. He explains a melting and shrinking Antarctica and a higher sea level could be felt around the world, but not uniformly. “It won't just be like a bathtub slowly filling up evenly around all of the coastlines,” Scambos said. “Almost unfairly, the areas that are losing ice the most will actually see sea level drop near those coasts.” Instead, some of the largest increase occurs “around the middle of the Earth, near the equator, on either side.” As I listened to him, I couldn’t stop thinking of Vietnam’s Mekong Delta, lying well within the region expected to feel the impact. 

Melting sea ice on one end of the planet is reshaping coastlines on another. And deep seawater intrusion in one river delta can ripple through food markets and dinner tables thousands of miles away. After seven years reporting on climate change, I have learned that while it’s tangled and complex, it’s not shapeless. It takes scientists, lawyers, farmers, and yes, journalists — as many perspectives as we can gather — to see the whole picture. Perhaps that’s what I have loved most about *Living on Earth*: its episodes patiently peel back one layer after another, revealing connections hidden underneath what first seem like local stories.

 

At *Living on Earth*, as I keep learning about our world, I’m also learning from our producers how to deliver more pieces of that understanding to listeners’ ears, whether while they are baking, sewing, commuting to work, or reporting on the environment themselves. Thank you for reading my journey. Until the next lesson, the next layer, the next story!

**Nhung Nguyen**

Editorial Intern
