## Blossoming Amidst the Wreckage

This week, [we’re rerunning a conversation](https://www.loe.org/shows/segments.html?programID=26-P13-00032\&segmentID=4) between Host Steve Curwood and author David George Haskell, whose book, [*How Flowers Made Our World: The Story of Nature's Revolutionaries*](https://bookshop.org/p/books/how-flowers-made-our-world-the-story-of-nature-s-revolutionaries-david-george-haskell/f48d853456b20df5?ean=9780593834961\&bkshp-astro=t) encourages listeners to immerse themselves in the enthralling world of flowering plants.

 

Funnily enough, around the same time I was producing this piece, I was also getting to know the very world Haskell describes.

 

It was mid-March, and I had just received an email that a plot had opened up in my hometown’s community garden. I had been on the waitlist for nearly two years, and had since moved out of my parents’ place in northern New Jersey to an apartment near Boston. But as I imagined my own garden plot – one I’d tend, harvest, and watch grow for years to come, I realized this was an opportunity I couldn’t pass up. I immediately called my dad to enlist his help, promising to visit often during the growing season. After some convincing, he reluctantly agreed, and we began to gather our supplies.

 

Neither of us had any experience gardening, and we unknowingly terrorized our 4-by-10 foot plot. We planted spicy pepper seedlings that died almost immediately, forgot to water our snap peas, and bought an absurd number of tomato plants without realizing how much space they needed to thrive.

 

The funny thing about gardening is that if you start talking about doing it, you’ll learn that almost everyone has some advice about how to turn your thumbs green. After recounting the tale of our dying peppers to our neighbor, she gave us some nasturtium seeds, an edible flower she claimed was quite easy to grow. We tossed them haphazardly between the peas and peppers, not thinking much of them.

 

A week later, my dad sent photos of the garden, and I hardly recognized the bed. Huge, round nasturtium leaves dotted the plot like fluffy polka dots. A month after that, the flowers arrived: blistering orange and brilliant yellow. They took over the garden, crowding out the perishing peppers and parched peas, hungry for every bit of sunlight, soil, and water. 

 

Suddenly, our garden was alive. The nasturtiums were resilient – even our tomato plants began to bloom, as if encouraged by their perseverance.

 

Then, as quickly as the flowers arrived, the floods came. 

 

An onslaught of sudden rain poured relentlessly for three days in late July. It drowned our backyard in three feet of water, flooded our garage, and even submerged our neighbor’s car. We spent hours trying to soak up the water with beach towels, but eventually, all we could do was wait it out.

 

This summer, the New York City metropolitan area has already seen [more flash flood warnings than any other year on record.](https://www.cnn.com/2026/07/29/weather/northeast-storm-flash-flood-threat-climate-hnk) The cause is, of course, climate change. [Warmer air is able to hold more moisture,](https://www.edf.org/why-are-floods-hitting-more-places-and-people) fueling heavier rainfall and flash flooding. Extreme heat and drought make it harder for the land to absorb water by drying out the soil and killing plant cover. And we haven’t yet seen the last of it – as the climate changes, these cycles are likely to become more intense.

 

I returned to the garden days later, expecting to find wreckage. But there they were: the flowers still standing strong, their shining blooms a banner of protest against the debris and mulch left behind by the storm. Our tomato plants had intertwined with bright purple morning glories, strengthening each other as they climbed skyward. 

 

It was a small testament to what Haskell explores in his book: flowers have been around for millions of years, enduring mass extinctions, fires, and floods – not only surviving, but thriving. There’s something comforting in knowing that these plants have faced adversity long before we humans set foot on Earth, and they may well outlast us, too. They’ll keep twisting and intertwining toward the sun, growing and shaping the world around them.
