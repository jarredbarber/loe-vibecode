## Keeping 'Moon Joy' Going, Months After Artemis II

April 2026 saw humans returning to the moon after more than 50 years since the Apollo missions. If you’re anything like me, you also felt like you were flying high after watching such a thrilling combination of scientific innovation, human achievement, and cosmic wonder. I was pretty tapped into Artemis II during the days of the mission – not only was I playing the NASA livestream footage every moment I could, but I also had the privilege of covering that beat for Living on Earth, where I got to speak to a [former NASA historian,](https://www.loe.org/shows/segments.html?programID=26-P13-00011\&segmentID=4) as well as an engineer/astronaut [who’s been to space twice](https://www.loe.org/shows/segments.html?programID=26-P13-00014\&segmentID=2), and the Artemis II [Lunar Science Lead.](https://www.loe.org/shows/segments.html?programID=26-P13-00033\&segmentID=2)

 

I wasn’t alone in my fascination. You might have heard the term “moon joy” thrown around when the mission was at its cultural peak. That phrase originated when, after seeing the Artemis II crew fully geeking out over seeing so many interesting lunar locations up close, [mission control science officer](https://www.threads.com/@nasa/post/DXKQIbpEU-v) Angela Garcia responded with “Copy, moon joy.” And then the phrase took off as other spectators asserted that yes, they were also experiencing astronomical levels of excitement. But it’s been months now since the safe return of the mission, and Artemis III won’t be ready until 2027 at the earliest. So, what do you do when you want to return to that state of ‘moon joy’?

 

If you’re looking to reexperience the mission, there are a few ways to go about it. NASA maintains a pretty comprehensive [YouTube page,](https://www.youtube.com/@NASA) so you can find the [livestream](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=z-j1uxBmis0) of the astronauts circling the moon (especially if you want to revisit the touching moment where the crew names one of the moon’s “bright spots” Carroll, after Commander Reid Wiseman’s late wife, and then they all get together to group hug it out, a moment which never fails to make me weep), or the [footage](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=nfhDuOHMp0A) of the crew landing back safe and sound (particularly good for when you need a reminder that sometimes, everything works out okay in the end). 

 

But if you’re looking for a throwback, I highly recommend the website [Apollo in Real Time](https://apolloinrealtime.org/), which syncs up audio, video, photos, transcripts, and even scientific data from the Apollo 11, 13, and 17 missions. If you listen around the date of one of the missions, say July 20 for Apollo 11’s moon landing, you can even join ‘in-progress’ to really relive the event. They line up the content from 1969 so that when it’s the to-the-second anniversary of Neil Armstrong’s first steps on the moon, you can watch along with the lunar lander footage and hear him speak that iconic “one small step” line. 

 

Now that’s pretty cool on its own, but if you’re looking to really dive deep, you can also read about the lunar rock samples Armstrong and Buzz Aldrin collected, or check on the audio and transcripts from each individual member of mission control as the mission went on.

![](https://mcusercontent.com/9f9ebdcfa232a532a7e70746b/images/bea33332-c9b8-15bd-5bd3-9b1e54ea302d.jpg)

 

Some other great sources of amazing imagery that can teach you more about the world we live in and beyond include the ALMA (the [Atacama Large Millimeter Array](https://www.almaobservatory.org/en/home/)) and the JWST ([the James Webb Space Telescope](https://www.loe.org/shows/segments.html?programID=22-P13-00027\&segmentID=5)). Last year, ALMA captured a [‘baby photo’ of a newly formed star](https://www.almaobservatory.org/en/press-releases/for-the-first-time-astronomers-witness-the-dawn-of-a-new-solar-system/attachment/alma-image-of-hops-315-a-still-forming-planetary-system/), one that’s very similar to the one we’re orbiting around, and JWST data showed evidence for the early stages of planetary formation around that star, giving us a glimpse of what our very own past might have looked like as our sun and Earth first developed.

 

I often reflect on why I’m so keen to work on these space stories. Sure, some of it comes from a lifelong fascination with space science (and a deep and abiding love of Star Trek), but I think these days, one of the things that keeps me coming back is the way space makes so much of the rest of life fall to the background. 

 

I recently traveled to upstate New York and Vermont, where the skies are darker and clearer than where I live in Boston, which was pretty lucky since my trip just so happened to line up with the yearly [Perseids](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Perseids) meteor shower. If you’ve never had the privilege of seeing a meteor shower, it’s quite the sight. I probably only saw a half-dozen “shooting stars,” but as I was watching the night sky, the Earth shrunk away and all I could think about was what else is out there beyond what we know. And with so much trouble and difficulty in our daily lives, escapism like that can be a necessary reset for my stressed-out brain.

 

But when it comes to space exploration, there’s another component that keeps me hooked: the incredible communal effort that’s put into making these missions happen. Sure, it’s great to see the amazing photos and learn the new science, but for me, a big part of what made Artemis II so compelling was the human aspect, where we saw how dedication, teamwork, and effort combined to make so many dreams into a reality. I loved learning about the global collaboration that made it all possible. A mission like this requires teamwork from top to bottom, from the crew aboard the ship to the third-grader who designed the ‘Rise’ [zero-gravity indicator](https://www.instagram.com/reels/DW38lK6jloT/), the scientists who investigated the safety of the heat shield to the divers collecting the astronauts after splashdown, the team who stocked [five different hot sauces](https://www.nasa.gov/missions/artemis/artemis-2/artemis-ii-whats-on-the-menu/) aboard the shuttle to the musicians who provided the daily [wake-up calls](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Artemis_II#Wake-up_calls)[.](https://www.instagram.com/reels/DW38lK6jloT/) And luckily, there’s a whole lot more to learn if you want to get another dose of ‘moon joy’ before we see Artemis III launch next year.
