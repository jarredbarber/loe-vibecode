## A Book Every Climate-Conscious Person Needs to Read

[The other week,](https://loe.org/shows/shows.html?programID=26-P13-00033) as part of our annual summer rerun series, we brought back our [conversation](https://www.loe.org/shows/segments.html?programID=26-P13-00033\&segmentID=3) with Dr. Hannah Ritchie, author of the book, [*Clearing the Air: A Hopeful Guide to Solving Climate Change in 50 Questions and Answers.&#xA0;*](https://bookshop.org/p/books/clearing-the-air-a-hopeful-guide-to-solving-climate-change-in-50-questions-and-answers-hannah-ritchie/3ab3b4f9a34ee2e1?ean=9780262052740\&affiliate=20556)

 

Dr. Ritchie is a data scientist. With a BSc in environmental geosciences, an MSc in carbon management, and a PhD in geosciences under her belt, she primarily researches the data behind these disciplines. Dr. Ritchie is now a senior researcher at the University of Oxford, Deputy Editor and Lead Researcher for [Our World in Data](https://ourworldindata.org/), and author of the Substack, [*By the Numbers*,](https://hannahritchie.substack.com/) with over 76,000 subscribers. She joined us for a live virtual event to discuss her book in March of this year. (You can catch the full webinar [here](https://www.loe.org/events/).) 

![](https://mcusercontent.com/9f9ebdcfa232a532a7e70746b/images/0244f9d3-0de4-9295-f01e-04e312773992.png)

 

I fell in love with Dr. Ritchie’s book for two main reasons. 

 

The first is the set up. 

 

The book is organized, as the title suggests, in question-and-answer format, divided by topics like “Renewable Energy,” “Electric Cars,” and “Minerals.” The 50 questions presented may have crossed your mind, whether you’re a climate scientist, an activist, or someone who’s just started learning about climate change. They may be questions you’ve been afraid to ask, or have assumed you should know the answer to. 

 

Some examples include:

 

*Won’t renewables and electric cars mean a lot more mining?*

 

*Don’t wind farms kill lots of birds and wildlife?*

 

*Aren’t our efforts pointless if China’s emissions keep growing?*

 

This premise—that all of us, regardless of our climate expertise, may still have remaining questions—feels like a fresh take to me. As a producer for LoE, I read countless books about our changing environment. I’ve found that many authors assume their readers understand the basic science behind the climate crisis, exempting them from addressing foundational concepts. Certain facts are no longer explained, like [why climate change is increasing the likelihood of extreme storms.](https://www.loe.org/shows/segments.html?programID=26-P13-00030\&segmentID=1) Certain questions are no longer entertained. 

 

Not only does this alienate readers who may be new to the climate space, but it also subtly shames climate pros for being unsure of something when they do have a question. In contrast, Dr. Ritchie’s book is devoid of pretension, and meets its readers where they are. In this way, it can be used as intended, as a guide, for all of us. 

 

Vernon Loeb, Executive Editor of our media partner, [*Inside Climate News*, ](https://insideclimatenews.org/)introduced Hannah during the live event. Vernon is a veteran climate reporter, and even he said he would keep the book on his desk for reference from now on. That’s quite an endorsement. 

 

I have followed in Vernon’s footsteps. I also keep *Clearing the Air&#x20;*&#x6F;n my desk. While I do gain expertise with each passing year in this field, I still have questions. Actually, I have questions all the time. And Dr. Ritchie’s book reminds me that that’s completely normal.

For many of us, data feels overwhelming. We’re often presented with cherry picked statistics, and it seems you can find a data point to support every argument. We’re left wondering, *well, what’s actually true?*

 

Dr. Ritchie quells this confusion by putting data *into context.* This is perhaps the most important lesson that I will remember from *Clearing the Air.&#xA0;*

 

When you see any statistic, she writes, there is one question you should ask:

 

*Compared to what?*

 

For example, you might have heard, “Wind turbines kill millions of birds.” As Dr. Ritchie explains, this is technically true. Across the US, wind turbines *do* kill about 1.2 million birds each year. But here’s where we pause and ask:

 

*Compared to what?*

 

Cats kill *2.4 billion* birds a year. Buildings kill another *600 million.&#x20;*&#x57;ind turbines kill a tiny fraction of birds compared to other hazards, when put into context. Context gives statistics meaning. By changing the context of the 1.2 million birds per year statistic, we change that stat’s meaning. 

 

I find Dr. Ritchie’s treatment of data so compelling because she’s not just presenting us with the data itself. She’s also teaching us a *skill* we need to interrogate statistics *all on our own.&#xA0;*

 

In my own job as a producer and reporter, this was a necessary reminder. Statistics are a near-constant part of dealing with environmental news. It’s my responsibility to present those statistics accurately and most importantly, in context. So *Compared to what?&#x20;*&#x77;ill now be burned in my brain forever. But it’s not just environmental radio producers Hannah is training here. She’s made her book relevant to everyone. 

 

We all are bombarded with information every day. Knowing how to look for context, to question what we see and hear, is essential. It’s a tool we can carry into the real world, beyond the pages of a single book.
