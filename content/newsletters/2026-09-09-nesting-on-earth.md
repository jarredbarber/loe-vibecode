## Nesting on Earth

During the month of August, Living on Earth airs a series of reruns to give the staff a chance to tackle bigger projects, take a break from the news, and enjoy the summer weather. Over the past few weeks, I’ve been editing an interview with the author [David Gessner](https://davidgessner.net/) about the rerelease of his book [*Return of the Osprey: A Season of Flight and Wonder*](http://bookshop.org/a/20556/9781501783746). 

 

I enjoy talking to David. He’s just old enough to be my dad, and he isn’t afraid to share wisdom from his career with me, which is useful for a 30-year-old wannabe writer. During our pre-interview, David spoke of how *Return of the Osprey* is in many ways about finding your place. 

 

The book centers on one year during which David observed ospreys as they nested, mated, and raised chicks on Cape Cod, Massachusetts. It was after he married his wife, but before his daughter was born. At the time, he thought that he would possibly spend the rest of his life on the Cape, especially as he watched the birds build their lives there.

 

This is ironic for two reasons. The first is that ospreys migrate. Once the chicks are grown, their parents fly South, usually sometime in August. And then, by mid-September or so the chicks themselves depart for South America. The chicks somehow know how to get there, despite having no guidance from their parents. Then, once they are ready to reproduce, they fly back up North, usually near the spot where they hatched. The ospreys don’t settle anywhere, not really.

 

The second reason is that shortly after the book was published, David was offered a tenure track position at the University of North Carolina Wilmington. His wife, also a writer, had become pregnant, and they couldn’t pass up the stability of a tenured position, so they left their Cape home. Since then, most of his books have been elaborate travel logs, and like the ospreys, he often returns to summer on the Cape. David isn’t the type of bird to build a single nest. 

 

During the pre-interview, the two of us discussed how he biked between osprey nests, exploring the area as he went. I made a crack about how that would never be me. I don’t know if he got the joke. Moments after I said it, it dawned on me that David might not know that I use a manual wheelchair. (Or he does and the joke just wasn’t funny.) I’ve gone hand cycling and adaptive mountain biking, but I’m not about to go biking through wetlands to watch birds. My relationship to place is mediated through a different set of wheels. 

 

Until I moved to Boston, I never felt connected to where I live. I grew up in a suburb about six miles from downtown Pittsburgh, PA, the last stop on Pittsburgh’s Rail (what passes for a subway system there). My mother was the executive director of the Pittsburgh chapter of the American Institute for Architects. By learning about the history of the city’s buildings, she learned about the place itself. She knows more about the city than anyone I’ve ever met. When I bring a new friend or partner to town, she insists on giving a guided tour, lecturing on the architecture, old steel mills, and ethnic neighborhoods as she drives. 

 

I never understood why my mom cared so much about Pittsburgh. Because of my disability, I didn’t have the freedom other kids did to explore, even during the year I spent there after college. I’ve never ridden the Rail. I’ve never wandered downtown. I’ve never even gone out drinking in Pittsburgh. 

 

It wasn’t as if the city had no influence on me – after all, I became interested in the environment after a trip to the zoo as a toddler. But outside of a few magical moments, and a loyalty to [Heinz Ketchup](https://www.heinz.com/our-story), Pittsburgh means little to me. It is a cardboard box — one that holds some of my most important memories and most of my family — but the box itself means nothing to me. On my college application, I responded to the essay prompt “where do you feel the most comfortable?” with “in my wheelchair.” 

 

After you work for Living on Earth for more than a few months, you learn that place matters. Whether I’m talking to a journalist who’s [fallen in love with the deep ocean](https://www.loe.org/shows/segments.html?programID=24-P13-00031\&segmentID=1) or [a scientist listening to the soil to learn more about the health of an ecosystem](https://loe.org/shows/segments.html?programID=25-P13-00033\&segmentID=1), I’m reminded that to be living *on* Earth is to live *in* a specific place on this planet. 

 

And my place is Boston. I’ve lived in the area for seven extremely chaotic years. Between a pandemic, multiple surgeries, and the general messy nature of being a twenty-something, Boston has seen me through some of the most difficult times of my life. Despite the madness, I’ve never felt more at home. Thanks to my driver’s license and Boston’s public transit system, much of the city feels familiar to me. I know where the best hills are for soaring down in my wheelchair. And where to slow down lest I flip over a crack in the road. I know where to grab a beer. And where to grab a latte the morning after. I know where to take someone on a first date. And where to cry if we break up. 

 

I am not David. While he builds nests in many different trees, I am more Hobbit-like. I love a grand adventure, but I tend to [burrow in one place](https://www.newzealand.com/us/plan/business/hobbiton-movie-set-tours/), and one place only. And while I don’t mimic the osprey’s migratory lifestyle, I do understand what it means to be part of a place, the same way the ospreys are a part of the Cape and its ecosystem. 

 

Life is unpredictable, so I make no promises that I’ll spend the rest of my days here. But I have found a place on this planet to call home. I have nested.

**El Wilson**

Producer
