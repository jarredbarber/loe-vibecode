## Transcript

SMITH: Scientists have been studying exoplanets, or planets beyond our solar system, for decades. But a 2026 study published in Nature Astronomy found that one exoplanet is unlike anything we’ve seen before. This planet is called L98-59d, and it’s about 1.6 times the size of Earth and 35 light years away from us. What makes it so special is that, unlike other exoplanets of a similar size and density, it’s neither a rocky planet surrounded by gas, nor a water world. It’s…mushy.

NICHOLLS: It's more like you see those videos of people like poking magma when they go visit Iceland or something like that, it's like a molasses, like a honey type viscosity, you could attempt to walk on it, and given the density of rock, you would not sink, but it would be like walking on molten lava.

![Dr. Harrison Nicholls is a postdoctoral researcher at the University of Cambridge’s Institute of Astronomy and co-author of the study. (Photo: Harrison Nicholls)](https://loe.org/content/2026-07-31/PLANET_nicholls.jpg)

SMITH: That’s Harrison Nicholls, a postdoctoral research assistant at the University of Cambridge and co-author of the study. He said scientists had known about this planet for years, but new observations from the James Webb Space Telescope revealed that its atmosphere is rich in hydrogen sulfide. In order to sustain such a volatile atmosphere, they concluded the planet must be in a liquid, molten state. Nicholls and his colleagues then used computer simulations to reconstruct the planet’s history. They found that L98-59d likely has a global magma ocean, hundreds of miles deep, and possibly a molten core as well. Planets form through an incredibly energy-intensive process, so young planets are extremely hot and cool over time. But a sulfur-rich atmosphere trapped this planet’s internal heat, preventing it from cooling. So it stayed in this molten, mushy state – even though it’s about 4.5 billion years old. And that hydrogen sulfide atmosphere would likely come with another defining feature: the overwhelming smell of rotten eggs. While this planet is almost certainly uninhabitable, Nicholls is excited about what this new discovery could mean.

NICHOLLS: I think we're going to keep in mind that there's going to be a big diversity of worlds out there, beyond anything we see in the solar system, because planets start molten, and that includes the earth, so by looking at these molten worlds, we can to some extent, look at Earth when it was just born.

SMITH: For Living on Earth, I’m Bella Smith.
