DOERING: Keep your ears peeled this fall for the sounds of animals as they go about preparing for winter. You might be surprised at just who is making those chirps and barks. BirdNote®’s Ariana Remmel reports.

BirdNote®  
Squirrel or Bird?  
Written by Conor Gearin

{% cue text="Fox Squirrel barks" %}

Identifying birds by ear means getting familiar with each species’ unique voice. And that means learning the other voices in the ecosystem, too. Like the Fox Squirrel’s barks:

{% cue text="Fox Squirrel barks" %}

This squirrel’s alarm barks could at first sound like the calls of a Red-bellied Woodpecker:

{% cue text="Red-bellied Woodpecker calls" %}

But with enough practice, you can hear how the squirrel’s barks are more throaty, and more frequent than the woodpecker. Here’s the squirrel again:

{% cue text="Squirrel barks" %}

And the woodpecker:

{% cue text="Red-bellied Woodpecker calls" %}

That’s not the only voice that could trip you up in the forest — take a listen to this cute little trill:

{% cue text="Eastern Chipmunk chip-trill call" %}

![Above, a fox squirrel climbs over a log in Asilomar, California. Over time, learning to distinguish the two can help birders appreciate all the wildlife around them. (Photo: Skyttea, Wikimedia Commons, CC BY-SA 3.0)](https://loe.org/content/2026-10-09/BIRDNOTE_foxsquirrel.jpg)

While it might sound a bit like a kingbird, it’s really the chip-trill call of a chipmunk, given when they’re telling another chipmunk to back off.

{% cue text="Eastern Chipmunk chip-trill call" %}

In comparison, Eastern Kingbirds sound more shrill, increasing in volume rather than decreasing as they continue:

{% cue text="Eastern Kingbird call" %}

Here’s the chipmunk again:

{% cue text="Eastern Chipmunk chip-trill call" %}

And here’s the kingbird:

{% cue text="Eastern Kingbird call" %}

Eventually, you might recognize each species’ voice, like recognizing a friend. All it takes is time.

{% cue text="Eastern Chipmunk chip-trill call" %}

I’m Ariana Remmel.
